Substitution

Draft. Lived experience, not medical advice. If a change of plan matters to you, talk it through with a clinician you trust.

Harm reduction is already familiar

You already practice it: switching to a lighter drink, spacing your drinks, a nicotine patch instead of cigarettes, calling a ride instead of driving after. Harm reduction doesn't need a new name. It needs honesty about what it's for.

Substitution is harm reduction too

Sometimes the useful change isn't "stop everything." It might be:

  • Frequency or amount — using less often, or less intensely
  • Supply — a more predictable, regulated supply where legally available
  • Setting — planned settings with trusted people instead of chaotic ones
  • Purpose — noticing what need the substance meets, and finding a different way to meet some of that need

People who use drugs have long practiced substitution — including cannabis in place of stimulants, opioids, or alcohol. Research documents that this happens; evidence that it works for a specific person is still developing. "Some people use this strategy" is supported. "This swap will be safer for everyone" is not.

From the founder's lived experience

Raccoon Diplomacy's founder moved away from cocaine use they no longer wanted without becoming abstinent from every substance. Their continuing relationship with LSD, psilocybin mushrooms, MDMA/molly, and cannabis helped shape the belief that choosing a less harmful path can be a real path out.

Lived experience, not a medical recommendation — no claim that these four substances are harmless.

A claim we can responsibly make

For some people, choosing LSD, psilocybin mushrooms, cannabis, or MDMA instead of a particular pattern involving cocaine, methamphetamine, injection, or alcohol can reduce total harm. The comparison belongs to the real person and pattern — not to a universal list declaring one drug "good" and another "bad."

There is no universal "safe-drug" ranking

Legal status and cultural acceptance do not reliably describe harm. Alcohol can cause profound harm; an illegal substance can be used in a pattern a person experiences as less harmful. Ask about the whole pattern: the substance, the route, the supply, how often and how much, the person, the setting, and the goal.
