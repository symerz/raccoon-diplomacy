Recognize, Respond, Stay

Draft, unreviewed by a qualified trainer. The full workbook is being reviewed. This is a summary, not training. If someone is in trouble now, call 911.

If someone might be overdosing

Don't wait for every sign. If an opioid overdose is possible, respond:

  • Won't wake or respond to voice or touch
  • Breathing very slow, irregular, gasping, snoring/gurgling, or absent
  • Lips, fingertips, or skin looking blue, gray, or pale
  • Body limp; pupils very small

Check, Call, Reverse, Breathe, Stay

  1. Check. Try to wake them. Look and listen for normal breathing.
  2. Call 911. Give the location, say the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally, follow the dispatcher's instructions.
  3. Reverse. Give naloxone per its instructions. It reverses opioids only — alcohol, benzos, and stimulants, no — but it generally won't harm if opioids aren't involved.
  4. Breathe. Support breathing or CPR per your training or the dispatcher. If breathing but unconscious, recovery position when safe.
  5. Stay. Another naloxone dose after 2–3 minutes if no response. Naloxone wears off while opioids remain in the body — stay until help arrives.

Don't: slap, ice baths, force walking, food or drink, or inject stimulants. They don't reverse an overdose and they waste time.

Other emergencies

Chest pain, seizure, collapse, severe overheating, stroke-like signs, or dangerous agitation → 911. Alcohol or sedative poisoning: don't assume someone can "sleep it off" — side-lying position, call 911. A frightening psychedelic experience usually needs calm, less noise, and a trusted person — call 911 if consciousness, breathing, or safety is in question.

The legal note is not a promise

Missouri (RSMo § 195.205) and Kansas both have limited-protection statutes for seeking medical help in a drug-related emergency. They are not blanket immunity. Calling early is still the right call.
